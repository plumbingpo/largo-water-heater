Keeping Your Dunedin Water Heater Running Longer

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, test the relief valve annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which reduces the risk of scalding and slows mineral buildup. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush once a year if their water is hard.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters run 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models usually last 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing symptoms like rusty hot water, rumbling during heating, moisture around the base, or showers that go cold sooner, a planned replacement is almost always cheaper and far less stressful than an emergency one after the tank lets go.

The earlier you report a hot water problem, the cheaper it usually is. A sediment layer that's just starting to rumble needs only a flush, yet that same buildup, ignored through a few more years, can overheat the bottom of the tank and shorten the life of the tank, forcing a replacement much sooner. Save our number on your phone today so you can reach us fast as soon as something seems off. A quick call saves time, money, and cold showers.

Common Water Heater Problems in Dunedin

Around Dunedin, water heater trouble usually trace back to a handful of causes. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be part of the picture: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, which produces the popping and rumbling you may hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

Age matters just as much. Many homes in the area still have a tank that was installed a decade or more ago, and once a tank is past about ten years, the anode rod has usually been consumed and rust begins attacking the steel itself. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, so our technicians handle everything from worn thermocouples and sooty burners to tripped high-limit switches and dead elements.

Setup matters too. A home on a private well can see more sediment and occasionally a sulfur smell in the hot water, which a different anode rod often cures. A tankless heater in hard water needs regular descaling. And an older installation being replaced often needs code items it never had: an expansion tank, a properly routed relief valve line, a drain pan, or seismic straps where required. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most are much cheaper to address early.
